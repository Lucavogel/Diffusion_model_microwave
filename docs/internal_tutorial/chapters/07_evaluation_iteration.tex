\chapter{Evaluation, Diagnosis, and Iteration}
\label{chap:evaluation}

Evaluation should answer a defined question about one frozen system. It should
not be mixed with checkpoint selection, gain tuning, or collection of new
demonstrations.

\section{Freeze the protocol}

Before reported trials, fix:

\begin{itemize}
  \item checkpoint and policy configuration;
  \item deployment command and safety limits;
  \item robot start state and gripper opening;
  \item camera placement, crop, and lighting;
  \item object/target distribution;
  \item trial success and failure rules;
  \item maximum time and permitted human intervention.
\end{itemize}

Development trials used to select these choices are excluded from the final
results. If a parameter changes, start a new evaluation block.

\section{Choose metrics that reveal task structure}

Task success is necessary but often insufficient. Divide the task into phases,
for example approach, alignment, grasp, extraction, transport, and release.
Record where the first unrecovered failure occurs, whether a re-grasp was
needed, whether contact occurred, and whether a safety stop triggered.

A 20-trial evaluation is useful for a preliminary engineering comparison, but
it does not establish broad robustness or a precise success probability.
Preserve individual trial records so later work can pool or stratify evidence.

\begin{projectbox}[Internship example]
Evaluation difficulty was increased from a simple physical pick-and-drop task
to constrained extraction with position and orientation variation. Each stage
used a newly frozen dataset/model pair, so the results measured feasibility at
that stage rather than zero-shot transfer of one unchanged policy.
\end{projectbox}

\section{Diagnose the layer that failed}

Classify evidence before changing the model:

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{0.24\textwidth}p{0.70\textwidth}@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Typical evidence and next check} \\
  \midrule
  \endhead
  Perception & Failure depends on lighting, occlusion, crop, or region; replay the exact policy images and compare coverage. \\
  Demonstration & Unnecessary corrections or inconsistent grasp timing appear in training episodes; curate or recollect targeted data. \\
  Model/training & Open-loop predictions are unstable across held-out episodes or seeds; inspect normalization, capacity, loss, and overfitting. \\
  Timing & Behavior degrades with speed or asynchronous delay; measure observation age and plan-switch discontinuity. \\
  Controller & Targets are reasonable but tracking overshoots or lags; inspect frames, gains, limits, and kinematics. \\
  Gripper/contact & Arm motion is correct but measured width or grasp differs; inspect command semantics, actuator delay, force, and collision. \\
  Hardware/sensor & Frames freeze or connections fail; repair the acquisition path before evaluating the policy. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\begin{methodbox}[Prefer the smallest informative experiment]
If one zone fails, compare that zone with another while freezing checkpoint,
lighting, and speed. If asynchronous execution is suspected, compare sync and
async at the same pose. A controlled pair of trials can be more informative
than immediately collecting another 50 demonstrations.
\end{methodbox}

\section{Improve data with human intervention}

When the policy fails in a reproducible state, a human can demonstrate the
missing correction and add it to a new dataset version. This is more targeted
than recollecting the entire task, but new data must be balanced so that the
policy does not forget common successful behavior. Human-in-the-loop and
intervention-based approaches formalize this idea
\citep{mandlekar2020hitl,ross2011dagger}.

Keep dataset versions immutable. Create a new version that records which
episodes were added, why they were added, and which evaluation failure motivated
them.

\section{Starting a new model or robot}

For a new policy architecture, preserve the dataset and evaluation protocol
first. Verify that the model accepts the same interface and compare parameter
count, training stability, inference latency, and task success. Architecture
comparison is not meaningful if observations, action horizon, or evaluation
conditions also change.

For a new robot, preserve the high-level benchmark but revalidate:

\begin{itemize}
  \item kinematic reachability and tool frame;
  \item camera geometry and occlusion;
  \item action/controller interface and timing;
  \item gripper semantics and force capability;
  \item workspace and safety limits;
  \item whether old demonstrations remain physically meaningful.
\end{itemize}

The most reusable artifacts are the benchmark definition, data schema, replay
tools, evaluation sheet, and staged deployment method. Robot-specific gains and
network addresses are not reusable scientific conclusions.

\section{Minimal handover record}

At the end of one experiment, leave:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a short task protocol;
  \item dataset path, episode count, and quality notes;
  \item resolved training configuration and source revision;
  \item selected checkpoint and measured inference latency;
  \item exact deployment command;
  \item trial-level evaluation table and representative videos;
  \item known failure cases and the next controlled experiment.
\end{enumerate}

This is enough for another student to understand what was tested, reproduce the
main result, and continue the investigation without repeating the full
development history.
